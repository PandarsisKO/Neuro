# Golden: Buying a Small Business — Masterplan

## Purpose

This project is trying to answer a single practical question: how does an individual buyer acquire a small main-street business — HVAC, services, or light manufacturing — in the United States using an SBA 7(a) loan? Specifically, it covers the financing structure and down-payment rules, how these businesses are valued, what due diligence must cover, how to structure the deal (LOI, working capital, seller transition), and what to do in the first 90 days after closing.

---

## Executive Summary

Buying a small main-street business with an SBA 7(a) loan is a well-defined process with clear rules at each stage. The SBA 7(a) program allows a buyer to acquire a business with as little as 10% down — and up to half of that injection can come from a seller note on full standby, reducing real cash needed to roughly 5% of purchase price. The loan carries a variable rate around prime plus 2.5–3%, matures in up to 10 years (25 years if real estate dominates), and requires the business's cash flow to cover debt service at a minimum 1.25x DSCR. Valuation for smaller main-street businesses is anchored to Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE), with most deals closing in a 2–4x multiple range; HVAC and trades are currently seeing upward pressure from private-equity roll-ups. Due diligence is the highest-leverage step, with assignability of contracts and leases, customer concentration, and verified add-backs being the most common deal-killers. The LOI must lock in an exclusivity window, anchor the SDE figure, and introduce a working capital peg before the exact number is determined. Closing day is operationally dense — bank accounts, payroll, insurance, and passwords must transfer the same day. The first 90 days should be spent listening, making small visible wins for employees, and avoiding any firings except for safety reasons.

## Key Insights

### 1. Financing Structure and Down Payment

**The 10% equity injection rule.** On a $1M purchase, the buyer must bring $100,000 in cash or equity to closing. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 0:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=6s)

**The seller-note half-stack.** Up to half of the 10% equity injection can be structured as a seller note instead of buyer cash, cutting real cash needed to approximately 5% of purchase price. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 0:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=27s)

**Full standby is the catch.** For the seller note to count toward the buyer's required equity, it must be put on full standby — zero principal or zero interest payments — for the entire SBA loan term, capped at half the injection. Most sellers refuse this condition. [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 2](file://report_sba_summary.pdf) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 45:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2743s)

**Rate.** SBA 7(a) rates run at roughly prime plus 2.5–3%, and lenders can differ by a full percentage point, so shopping multiple lenders is material. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 0:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=47s) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 49:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2991s)

**Loan ceiling.** Deals larger than the SBA 7(a) program cap require conventional financing or a blended structure. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=75s)

**Maturity.** Standard maturity is up to 10 years; if real estate is the largest deal component, maturity can extend to 25 years, which significantly lowers monthly payments. [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 2](file://report_sba_summary.pdf) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 58:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=3494s) — timeline extends further when real estate is involved. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=104s)

**Guarantee percentages.** The SBA guarantees 85% of loans at or below $150,000 and 75% of loans above that amount. [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 1](file://report_sba_summary.pdf)

**Guarantee fee.** A guarantee fee is charged on the guaranteed portion of the loan, varies by loan size and maturity, and can be rolled into the financed loan amount rather than paid out of pocket. [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 3](file://report_sba_summary.pdf) and [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=118s)

**Collateral.** Lenders must take whatever collateral is available, including the acquired business's assets. A collateral shortfall alone cannot kill the loan, but business assets must be pledged first. [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 3](file://report_sba_summary.pdf)

**Personal guarantee.** Anyone owning 20% or more of the business is personally liable with no cap — a key risk factor to weigh before signing the LOI. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=60s)

**Industry experience requirement.** Lenders typically will not approve a loan unless the buyer or an existing manager has relevant industry background. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=98s)

**The preferred blended structure.** The deal structure presented as optimal in the primary walkthrough source combines SBA 7(a) debt, a seller note on standby, and buyer cash equity. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 41:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2503s)

**Contrarian view — full seller financing.** One practitioner argues a buyer can structure a deal with zero percent down by using full seller financing instead of a bank loan, targeting a "tired" owner willing to accept full asking price in exchange for payment terms spread across 5–7 years, funded by the business's own cash flow. He states he has never used and will never use an SBA loan, citing the personal guarantee requirement and a roughly three-month paperwork timeline as dealbreakers. [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 0:09](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=9), [@ 0:21](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=21), and [@ 0:33](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=33)

### 2. Business Valuation

**SDE defined.** Seller's Discretionary Earnings equals net profit plus owner salary, benefits, and personal or one-time expenses run through the business. SDE is the correct metric for smaller main-street deals. [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**The SDE multiple range.** Most main-street businesses valued on SDE sell in a 2–4x range; anything above 4x requires special justification. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 33:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1995s) and [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**What moves the multiple.** Assignable contracts, an in-place general manager, and reviewed financials command the top of the range. Owner-dependent relationships, cash sales, and shoebox receipts push a business to the bottom or below the range. Revenue transferability, owner dependence, and books cleanliness are the primary levers. [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**The shift to EBITDA.** Once a business has professional management and over roughly $1M in earnings, buyers must factor in paying a replacement manager, so valuation shifts from SDE to EBITDA. Businesses with more than $1M of EBITDA typically trade in a higher multiple range. [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**HVAC/trades multiple inflation.** Private equity roll-ups are bidding up HVAC and plumbing valuations well above the commonly cited 2–3x earnings benchmark, and one advisor argues sellers in these trades should not settle for the traditional lower multiple. [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 0:49](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=49) and [@ 1:00](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=60)

**The DSCR check as a valuation ceiling.** An SBA lender independently checks that business cash flow covers loan payments at a minimum 1.25x DSCR and will reject a price as too high for SBA financing if it fails — regardless of comparable sales. Cash flow must cover the loan payment with 25% cushion for the deal to underwrite. [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 2](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued), [SBA 7(a) acquisition lending summary @ p. 3](file://report_sba_summary.pdf), and [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=86s)

### 3. Due Diligence

**Quality of Earnings (QoE) report.** A QoE verifies the seller's discretionary earnings by tying tax returns to bank statements; it typically costs $8k–$20k for businesses under $5M in revenue. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 0:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=17s)

**Contrarian cost-cutting on QoE.** One practitioner substitutes a bookkeeper's review of bank statements for a full QoE report, saving approximately $15,000. This is explicitly described as diverging from standard due-diligence practice. [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 1:08](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=68)

**Add-back scrutiny.** Sellers often add back salary, truck, and cell phone to inflate earnings. Only expenses that genuinely vanish when the seller leaves should count. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 0:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=40s)

**Customer concentration — the hard line.** If one customer exceeds 20% of revenue, that risk must be priced into the deal or the buyer should walk. Request three years of customer revenue data and check churn beyond the top ten. Both mainstream and contrarian sources agree this is a hard dealbreaker. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=64s) and [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 1:20](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=80)

**Assignability of contracts, leases, and licenses.** Every lease, contract, and license must be checked for assignability. This issue has ended more deals than bad financials. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=111s)

**Talk to customers before closing.** With the seller's permission, contact customers before closing to validate the business independently of seller-provided numbers. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=105s)

**Working capital — the forgotten item.** First-time buyers often close and discover an empty bank account. A normal working capital peg is trailing twelve-month average accounts receivable plus inventory minus accounts payable. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=81s)

**AR transfer is not automatic.** Accounts receivable transfer is not standard and must be negotiated separately deal by deal. [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 2](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**Core lender documents.** The lender will ask for a specific package of documents as part of underwriting. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 56:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=3376s)

### 4. Deal Structure: LOI, Working Capital, and Seller Transition

**Sourcing — off-market is better.** The best deals come from cold outreach to older owners rather than listings on sites like BizBuySell. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 12:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=738s)

**Buyer profile.** The outreach profile should state who you are, what you're looking for, and how you'll finance the purchase. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 16:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=995s)

**LOI exclusivity window.** The exclusivity window is meant to be long enough to complete diligence while preventing the seller from shopping the offer elsewhere. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 25:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1500s)

**Anchor SDE in the LOI.** Explicitly stating the SDE figure the offer is based on prevents later disputes over valuation. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 29:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1753s)

**Working capital peg in the LOI.** The concept of a working capital peg (normal receivables plus inventory minus payables) should be written into the LOI before the exact number is determined — this stops a seller from stripping out cash by collecting all receivables right before closing. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 37:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2255s) and [How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 2](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**Seller transition period.** The seller should stay on for a typical 90-day transition period, with this requirement and a holdback written into the purchase agreement. The agreement should spell out the seller's committed hours per week. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 2:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=124s) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:06:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4005s)

**Closing day mechanics.** The day itself is signing, the lender wiring funds, and the seller handing over keys and passwords. Bank signers, merchant account, payroll admin, and insurance should all transfer to the new owner the same day as closing — not delayed. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:02:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=3753s) and [@ 1:10:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4252s)

### 5. First 90 Days

**Listen to employees first.** Ask each employee what they would fix, then immediately fix whichever suggestion is cheapest to implement. This builds trust and surfaces operational knowledge quickly. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:19:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4762s)

**No firings — with one exception.** Do not fire anyone in the first 90 days; the exception is made only for a safety issue. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:23:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=5021s)

## Recommended Actions / How to Use These Insights

1. **Qualify yourself for SBA financing first.** Confirm you have at least 5–10% of anticipated purchase price in liquid cash, that you (or a retained manager) have relevant industry experience, and that you understand the personal guarantee obligation before approaching any lender. Use multiple SBA lenders to compare rates and closing timelines, as these can differ meaningfully. [SBA 7(a) loans for buying a business, explained @ 1:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00001&t=98s) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 49:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2991s)

2. **Source off-market.** Prioritize cold outreach to owners of businesses you can verify are aging and owner-operated. Build a simple one-page buyer profile stating your identity, acquisition criteria, and financing capability before reaching out. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 12:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=738s) and [@ 16:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=995s)

3. **Run a DSCR check before writing any LOI.** Use the business's trailing financials and your estimated SBA payment to confirm the deal clears 1.25x DSCR at your proposed price. If it does not, you either negotiate price down or the bank will reject the loan regardless of what you agree with the seller.

4. **Build the LOI around three anchors: SDE figure, exclusivity window, and working capital peg concept.** Do not leave any of these three to be "figured out later" — each is a source of post-LOI renegotiation if left vague. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 29:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1753s), [@ 25:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1500s), and [@ 37:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=2255s)

5. **Prioritize assignability and customer concentration in diligence.** These two issues kill more deals than financial discrepancies. Check every contract, lease, and license for assignability clauses, and pull three years of customer revenue data to test concentration. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=111s) and [@ 1:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=64s)

6. **Commission a QoE or bankable equivalent.** Budget $8k–$20k for a QoE that ties tax returns to bank statements, and scrutinize every add-back to confirm it disappears when the seller leaves. If cost is a constraint, understand that the cut-rate alternative (bookkeeper bank-statement review) is a known deviation from standard practice. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 0:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=17s) and [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 1:08](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=68)

7. **Negotiate the seller transition into the purchase agreement with specificity.** Define hours per week, duration (typically 90 days), and tie a meaningful holdback to the seller's completion of the transition period. [Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 2:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=124s) and [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:06:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4005s)

8. **Treat closing day as an operational event, not just a legal one.** Prepare a same-day checklist covering bank signers, merchant accounts, payroll administration, insurance, and password handover so nothing is left in the seller's control overnight. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:10:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4252s)

9. **Spend the first 90 days listening, not restructuring.** Survey every employee, implement the cheapest suggestion immediately, and defer all personnel decisions except safety-related ones. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 1:19:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=4762s) and [@ 1:23:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=5021s)

10. **For HVAC/trades specifically, validate valuation against current PE-driven comps.** The traditional 2–3x SDE benchmark may be stale. Understand what roll-up buyers are paying in your target market before anchoring your offer, and factor this into seller expectations. [No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 0:49](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=49)

## Open Questions and Gaps

- **Exact SBA 7(a) loan cap.** The sources confirm the cap exists and that deals above it require conventional or blended structures, but the specific dollar ceiling is not stated in the available findings. This number is essential for deal sizing and should be verified against current SBA program guidelines.

- **Specific lender document list.** The sources note that core documents are required for underwriting but do not enumerate them. A complete checklist of lender-required documents is missing. [Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 56:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=3376s)

- **EBITDA multiple range for larger businesses.** The sources state that businesses above ~$1M earnings trade on EBITDA at higher multiples, but the specific EBITDA multiple range is not provided. This is relevant for anyone targeting a business near or above that threshold.

- **Current SBA guarantee fee schedule.** The sources confirm the fee exists, varies by size and maturity, and can be financed — but do not provide the actual fee table. This figure affects total loan cost and should be sourced from current SBA guidelines.

- **Seller financing mechanics and legal structure.** The contrarian full-seller-financing approach is described conceptually but the legal documentation (promissory note terms, security interest, default provisions) is not addressed. Buyers exploring this path need additional sourcing.
