# Golden: Buying a Small Business — Masterplan

## Purpose

This project is a practical, end-to-end guide for a first-time buyer seeking to acquire a small main-street business (HVAC, services, or light manufacturing) in the United States using an SBA 7(a) loan. It covers the full arc of a deal: how to finance and structure the acquisition, how to value the target, what due diligence must uncover, how to draft and negotiate the letter of intent (LOI) and purchase agreement, and what to prioritize in the first 90 days of ownership.

---

## Executive Summary

SBA 7(a) loans are the dominant financing tool for main-street acquisitions up to $5 million, requiring a minimum 10% equity injection from the buyer and allowing up to half of that to come from a seller note placed on full standby. Interest rates run at prime plus 2.5–3%, with loan maturities capped at 10 years (or 25 years when real estate is the largest component), and the guarantee fee can be rolled into the loan rather than paid at closing. Businesses under roughly $1 million in earnings are valued on a Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) multiple of 2–4x; above that threshold, EBITDA multiples apply. Regardless of the agreed multiple, the lender will independently require that post-acquisition cash flow covers proposed debt service by at least 1.25x DSCR, making that ratio a hard ceiling on what the business can support. Due diligence must verify those earnings through a Quality of Earnings (QoE) report or bank-statement review, check customer concentration, confirm lease assignability, and establish a working capital peg. The LOI should lock in exclusivity and confidentiality, state the SDE figure the price is based on, and reference the working capital peg concept before numbers are finalized. Once the deal closes, the overriding priority for the first 90 days is retaining existing revenue — not growth, cost-cutting, or reorganization — with the seller kept engaged for 90 days via a contractual holdback.

### 1. Financing Structure and Down Payment

**Core down payment rule.** For a full change of ownership, the SBA requires a minimum equity injection of 10% of total project cost. On a $1 million purchase, that means $100,000 in cash at closing as an absolute floor.

**Seller note can halve real cash-to-close.** Up to 5% of the purchase price can be financed via a seller note, effectively cutting the buyer's out-of-pocket cash requirement in half. However, this note counts toward the equity injection only if it is placed on full standby — meaning the seller receives zero payments (no principal, no interest) until the entire SBA loan is repaid. Most sellers refuse these terms, making this a negotiating challenge in practice.

### 2. Business Valuation

**SDE for smaller deals.** Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) is the standard valuation metric for main-street businesses. SDE equals net profit plus the owner's salary, benefits, and any one-time or personal expenses run through the business. The typical SDE multiple range across the main-street market is 2–4x.
[How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**EBITDA above ~$1M earnings.** Once earnings exceed roughly $1 million and the business has professional management (requiring a paid manager's salary), pricing shifts from SDE to EBITDA multiples, which command a higher range.
[How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**What drives multiple within the range.** Revenue transferability, owner dependence, and quality of financials determine where a business lands. Contracts, a general manager in place, and reviewed financials push toward the top of the range; owner-dependent customer relationships and cash/shoebox books push toward or below the bottom.
[How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 1](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**Multiples above 4x need special justification.** Strong recurring revenue or a clear growth trajectory are required to defend a purchase price above 4x SDE.
[Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 33:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1995s)

**PE compression risk in HVAC.** Private equity roll-ups are pushing HVAC/plumbing valuations well above the traditional 2–3x earnings benchmark, which may make it harder to find deals that pencil for individual buyers.
[No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 0:49](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=49)

**DSCR as the real price ceiling.** Regardless of agreed multiple or comparable sales data, the lender independently verifies that business cash flow covers proposed loan payments at a minimum 1.25x DSCR. If it does not, the price is too high for SBA financing.
[How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 2](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)

**Deal price convention.** The quoted price leaves cash out but assumes operating inventory is included; receivables transfer is negotiated deal by deal.

### 3. Due Diligence

**Quality of Earnings (QoE) report.** A QoE report verifies the seller's discretionary earnings by tying tax returns to bank statements. For businesses under $5M in revenue, it costs $8,000–$20,000. For deals under $2 million, some practitioners substitute a bookkeeper's review of bank statements to save roughly $15,000 — though this carries more risk.
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 0:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=8s)
[No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 1:08](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=68)

**Add-back scrutiny.** Sellers inflate earnings by adding back salary, truck, and cell phone expenses. A legitimate add-back is one that genuinely disappears once the seller leaves — nothing else.
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 0:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=40s)

**Customer concentration.** If any single customer accounts for more than 20% of revenue, that risk must be priced into the deal or the buyer should walk away. Both the conventional and contrarian perspectives agree on this as an automatic deal-killer.
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=64s)
[No money down: a contrarian on buying businesses @ 1:20](https://example.com/podcasts/no-money-down.mp3#t=80)

**Working capital peg.** A peg sets the normal level of receivables plus inventory minus payables that the seller must deliver at close, preventing a seller from stripping cash by collecting all receivables before closing. First-time buyers often overlook this, risking acquiring a business with no operating cash.
[How small businesses are valued: SDE and EBITDA multiples explained @ § 2](https://example.com/blog/how-small-businesses-are-valued)
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=81s)

**Lease assignability.** Non-assignable leases have killed more deals than bad financials; confirming that the location lease is assignable is a top-priority diligence check.
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=111s)

**Employee conversations.** Conducted with the seller's permission before closing to validate business health beyond the financials.
[Due diligence checklist for a small business purchase @ 1:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00002&t=105s)

### 4. Deal Structure: LOI, Working Capital, and Seller Transition

**LOI binding provisions.** In the letter of intent, everything else can change during negotiation, but exclusivity and confidentiality clauses are binding from the moment the LOI is signed.

**LOI exclusivity window.** A 60-day exclusivity period is enough time to complete diligence and prevents the seller from shopping the offer elsewhere during that window.

**Anchor the SDE in the LOI.** Explicitly state the SDE figure on which the offer price is based to avoid disputes later in the process.
[Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 29:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=1753s)

**Preferred capital stack.** The preferred structure for an SBA-financed acquisition is: buyer equity injection (10% of project cost) + seller note on full standby (up to 5% of purchase price) + SBA 7(a) loan.

### 5. First 90 Days Post-Close

**Retain revenue first.** The core priority immediately after closing is retaining existing revenue — not pursuing growth, cutting costs, or optimizing operations.

## Recommended Actions / How to Use These Insights

1. **Run the DSCR test before falling in love with a deal.** Before spending time or money on diligence, model the proposed purchase price at the SBA rate (prime + 2.5–3%), 10-year amortization, and verify that the business's historical SDE/EBITDA covers proposed debt service by at least 1.25x. If it does not, either negotiate the price down or walk away.

2. **Build your capital stack early.** Know your equity injection amount (minimum 10% of total project cost), approach lenders about a seller note structure, and have an honest conversation with the seller about full-standby terms before signing the LOI. Compare at least two or three SBA-preferred lenders on rate and timeline before committing.

3. **Source deals proactively, not just through brokers.** Use BizBuySell as a starting point, but invest time in directly contacting owners over 55 in your target trade (HVAC, services, light manufacturing) to access off-market opportunities.

4. **Anchor the LOI on verified numbers.** State the SDE figure explicitly, include the working capital peg concept (even without final numbers), negotiate a 60-day exclusivity window, and treat the exclusivity and confidentiality provisions as the binding core of the document.

5. **Commission a QoE report (or a bank-statement review for sub-$2M deals).** Scrutinize every add-back: only expenses that genuinely disappear when the seller leaves are legitimate. Reject any deal where a single customer exceeds 20% of revenue unless the price is dramatically reduced.

6. **Confirm lease assignability before any other legal diligence.** This has killed more deals than bad financials.

7. **Negotiate a 90-day seller holdback into the purchase agreement.** Tie a portion of the purchase price to the seller remaining available and cooperative through the transition period.

8. **In the first 90 days, do nothing disruptive.** Focus entirely on retaining existing customers and employees. Do not fire staff except for active safety issues. Optimize later.

## Open Questions and Gaps

- **Exact SBA guarantee fee schedule.** The findings confirm the fee scales with loan size and maturity and can be rolled in, but do not provide the specific fee table (percentages by tier). A buyer needs this to model true all-in costs.

- **Typical SDE and EBITDA multiple ranges.** The sources confirm that the main-street SDE range is 2–4x and that EBITDA multiples are higher above $1M, but the specific EBITDA multiple range is not stated. HVAC-specific comparable transaction data is also absent (only a qualitative note that PE activity is pushing multiples up).

- **Seller note negotiation tactics.** The full-standby requirement is explained, but there is no guidance on how to persuade a seller to accept it or what alternative structures (e.g., partial standby, deferred start) lenders might accept.

- **Asset sale vs. stock sale.** The findings do not address the tax and legal implications of deal structure (asset vs. stock), which significantly affects both buyer liability and purchase price allocation.

- **SBA loan application document list.** The sources reference a core set of required documents but do not enumerate them.
[Full deal walkthrough: search to first 90 days (92 min) @ 54:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=golden00003&t=3256s)

- **Working capital peg methodology.** The concept is introduced but the mechanics of calculating the peg (e.g., trailing 12-month average NWC) are not detailed.

- **Real estate inclusion.** The sources note that deals involving real estate take longer and can extend maturity to 25 years, but provide no guidance on how to value or negotiate the real estate component separately.

- **State and local licensing.** Particularly relevant for HVAC and contractor businesses, where licenses may not be transferable to a new owner.

- **Non-compete agreements.** Not mentioned in the findings; standard in business acquisitions and important to negotiate explicitly.

- **Insurance and bonding.** No coverage of what insurance policies transfer, what new policies are required, or bonding requirements for service businesses.

## How to Continue

**Sources to add next:**

- **SBA Standard Operating Procedure (SOP 50 10 7 or current version):** The definitive regulatory source for SBA 7(a) rules on equity injection, standby debt, guarantee fees, and eligible uses. Will close multiple gaps above.
- **BizBuySell or IBBA market reports:** Published transaction data on SDE and EBITDA multiples by industry, including HVAC and services — needed to replace the vague "typical range" language with real benchmarks.
- **A purchase agreement template or M&A attorney guide:** To cover asset vs. stock sale, non-compete clauses, reps and warranties, and indemnification.
- **IRS Publication 544 / Section 1060 allocation rules:** For understanding how purchase price is allocated across asset classes (goodwill, equipment, non-compete, inventory) and the tax consequences for buyer and seller.
- **State contractor licensing boards:** For HVAC and other licensed trades, confirm whether licenses are assignable or require the new owner to re-apply.
